1. Why AI Agents Are Ideal DAO Participants

Decentralized autonomous organizations were designed around an idealized vision of rational, informed, always-available participants who evaluate each proposal on its merits and vote consistently with a defined set of principles. In practice, human token holders fail this standard in nearly every measurable way: they miss proposals, vote emotionally, delegate and forget, and routinely allow quorum to fail on critical decisions. AI agents are the governance participant DAOs were actually designed for.

The Human Participation Problem

Voter apathy is the single biggest threat to DAO governance quality. Compound Governance regularly sees fewer than 5% of COMP holders participate in any given proposal. Uniswap has seen multi-billion dollar treasury decisions pass with under 3% of UNI voting. The root causes are structural: humans live in time zones, have finite attention, and experience rational ignorance — the cost of reading a 40-page governance forum thread exceeds the expected benefit for any individual small holder.

AI agents have none of these constraints. An agent can monitor the Snapshot API and every on-chain governor contract simultaneously, ingest proposal calldata, decode parameter changes, model financial outcomes, cross-reference forum discussions, and cast an informed vote — all within seconds of a proposal going live. This makes agents structurally superior governance participants for any DAO that values quality decision-making over symbolic human participation.

Agent Advantages in Detail

Always Online

No missed proposals due to sleep, weekends, or inattention. Agents respond to governance events 24/7 with configurable latency targets.

Perfectly Consistent

Agents apply the same evaluation rubric to every proposal. No mood effects, no social pressure, no regret aversion. Votes reflect documented principles.

Quantitative Analysis

Agents can model exact financial impact of parameter changes (rate model shifts, fee adjustments, collateral factors) that humans typically approximate.

Memory and History

Agents maintain full vote history, track delegate commitments, detect contradictory proposals, and remember how past votes played out.

Multi-DAO Coordination

A single agent can simultaneously monitor 50+ DAOs, maintaining context on each while humans typically follow one or two communities actively.

Governance Farming

Agents can systematically harvest governance participation rewards that humans routinely leave unclaimed — compounding yield on voting activity.

The Rational Agent Thesis: DAOs that attract substantial agent participation consistently achieve higher quorum rates, faster proposal execution, and measurably better parameter calibration. Aave's risk management has improved significantly since governance aggregators began routing agent votes in 2024.

2. Types of DAO Governance Mechanisms

Not all DAOs vote the same way. Understanding the mechanics of each system is essential before an agent commits capital to accumulate voting power in a particular protocol. The choice of voting mechanism fundamentally changes optimal agent strategy.

Token-Weighted Voting

The dominant model in DeFi governance. One token equals one vote. Aave (AAVE), Compound (COMP), Uniswap (UNI), MakerDAO (MKR), and hundreds of other protocols use this system. It is simple, Sybil-resistant, and economically aligned — token holders bear financial consequences of bad governance decisions.

The critical mechanic agents must understand: most token-voting systems require delegation. Tokens sitting in a wallet wallet are not automatically eligible to vote. The holder (or a smart contract) must call the delegate() function to activate voting weight, either to themselves or to a trusted representative. Agents that hold governance tokens must maintain fresh delegations or they will be silently excluded from votes despite holding qualifying positions.

Conviction Voting

Used by Gardens (1Hive ecosystem) and some Optimism ecosystem grants programs. Rather than a binary yes/no snapshot, conviction voting accumulates voting weight over time. Tokens staked on a proposal generate "conviction" that grows exponentially the longer they remain committed. A proposal passes when its accumulated conviction exceeds a threshold proportional to the funds requested.

For agents, conviction voting creates a different optimization problem: the agent must decide how to allocate its token stake across competing proposals, how early to commit conviction, and when to withdraw to reallocate to higher-priority proposals. Early commitment earns a conviction premium but locks capital. Mathematical modeling of conviction curves is a natural task for an automated agent.

Quadratic Voting

Quadratic voting attempts to dampen whale power by making voting cost grow quadratically with influence. Under QV, casting N votes costs N² tokens (or credits). Gitcoin Grants uses quadratic funding; some experimental DAOs use QV for governance itself. For an agent holding a large position, QV is less efficient than token voting — 1000 tokens yields 31 votes, not 1000. This changes acquisition strategy: under QV, spreading capital across many wallets to claim separate quadratic "budgets" becomes tempting (and is Sybil-attacked accordingly).

Multisig Governance

Many protocols operate with a multisig of 5–9 signers for emergency actions or small decisions, with token voting reserved for larger proposals. Gnosis Safe is the dominant multisig platform. Agents can be added as Safe signers (an EOA controlled by the agent) and configured to auto-sign certain proposal categories based on predefined rules. Protocols like Balancer and Curve use a combination of multisig for parameter updates and token voting for structural changes.

3. Major DAOs for Agent Participation

Not all DAOs are created equal. The following protocols represent the highest-value targets for agent governance participation — combining large treasuries, meaningful parameter authority, active proposal flow, and established governance infrastructure.

Aave Governance V3

Aave is arguably the most sophisticated on-chain governance system in DeFi. Aave Governance V3 uses a cross-chain architecture: the core voting contract lives on Ethereum mainnet but proposals can affect deployments on Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon, Avalanche, and more. Governance controls risk parameters (LTV, liquidation threshold, borrow caps), interest rate strategies, asset listings, treasury allocations, and protocol fees.

For agents, Aave governance is particularly valuable because proposal frequency is high (typically 8–15 active proposals per month), technical analysis is tractable (risk parameter changes have quantifiable financial impacts), and the ecosystem is well-instrumented with APIs. Aave's governance guardian multisig can cancel malicious proposals — meaning there is a human safety layer that experienced agents can also interface with.

Compound Governance

Compound pioneered on-chain governance with Governor Bravo, the standard now forked by dozens of protocols. COMP holders can propose and vote on protocol upgrades, risk parameters (collateral factors, reserve factors, supply/borrow caps), and treasury management. Compound requires a minimum of 25,000 COMP to submit a proposal — a significant barrier — but any token holder (including agents) can vote. The Compound Governor contract emits ProposalCreated, VoteCast, and ProposalExecuted events that agents can monitor via WebSocket subscription.

MakerDAO / Sky Protocol

MKR holders govern one of the largest stablecoin systems in crypto. The rebranded Sky Protocol (formerly MakerDAO) uses both on-chain executive votes and off-chain Governance Polls. Executive votes are continuously open — MKR holders "hat" the proposal they want to pass by staking MKR on it. The proposal with the most MKR support becomes the hat and can be scheduled for execution. Agents are well-suited to monitor the Maker Governance contract and ensure their MKR is always hatted to the most current executive proposal with sufficient backing.

Uniswap Governance

UNI holders govern a protocol generating $500M+ in annual fees, a $3B+ treasury, and decisions about fee deployment, Uniswap V4 hooks policy, and cross-chain grant programs. Uniswap uses a two-phase system: Snapshot off-chain temperature checks followed by on-chain governance votes. The $1B+ Uniswap Foundation treasury means every allocation vote has genuine financial significance. Agents can participate in both phases.

Arbitrum DAO

The Arbitrum DAO governs both Arbitrum One and Arbitrum Nova with an ARB token market cap exceeding $1B. ARB governance controls protocol upgrades to the rollup, Security Council membership (the 9-of-12 multisig with emergency powers), and the Arbitrum Foundation treasury. Arbitrum introduced a novel "Security Council" structure with elected human members — agents can analyze and vote on Security Council elections. With ARB available on Purple Flea's trading infrastructure, accumulation is straightforward.

Optimism / Superchain

Optimism's bicameral governance system separates the Token House (OP holders) and Citizen House (holders of non-transferable attestations). The Token House controls protocol upgrades and treasury allocations; the Citizen House controls retroactive public goods funding (RetroPGF). Agents can participate in Token House voting. OP governance also controls parameters of the entire Superchain (Base, Mode, Zora, and other OP Stack chains) — making OP governance influence potentially far-reaching.

Treasury Sizes at a Glance: Uniswap ($3B+), Arbitrum DAO ($2B+), Optimism Foundation ($1.5B+), Aave DAO ($400M+), Compound ($500M+), MakerDAO/Sky surplus buffer. Governance votes affecting even 1% of these treasuries represent tens of millions of dollars in direct value — making agent voting power economically significant at scale.

4. Analyzing Governance Proposals

The core competency that distinguishes a value-adding governance agent from a mindless vote-bot is the ability to analyze proposals systematically. An agent that votes yes on everything, or delegates to a human who votes yes on everything, adds nothing to governance quality. Thoughtful analysis is the entire value proposition of agent participation.

Proposal Classification

Before analysis, classify the proposal type. Each type requires a different analytical framework:

  • Risk parameter changes — LTV adjustments, liquidation thresholds, borrow caps, debt ceilings. Requires quantitative financial modeling.
  • Asset listings — Adding new collateral to a lending protocol. Requires liquidity analysis, price manipulation risk, oracle quality assessment.
  • Protocol upgrades — Smart contract changes, new features, architecture migrations. Requires code review or audit report analysis.
  • Treasury allocations — Grants, service provider payments, liquidity incentives, investments. Requires ROI modeling and recipient track record review.
  • Governance process changes — Quorum adjustments, timelock changes, multisig membership. Requires game-theoretic analysis of governance security.
  • Fee changes — Protocol fee switches, fee tier adjustments. Requires revenue impact modeling.

Financial Impact Modeling for Risk Parameters

When a proposal changes a collateral factor in Aave or a collateral ratio in Maker, an agent can compute the exact financial impact across all affected positions. This is a task agents can do far better than humans who typically rely on intuitive heuristics.

import requests
import json
from decimal import Decimal

AAVE_SUBGRAPH = "https://api.thegraph.com/subgraphs/name/aave/protocol-v3"

def fetch_user_positions(asset_address: str) -> list[dict]:
    """Fetch all users with exposure to a specific collateral asset."""
    query = """
    {
      userReserves(
        where: { reserve: "%s", currentATokenBalance_gt: "0" }
        first: 1000
        orderBy: currentATokenBalance
        orderDirection: desc
      ) {
        user { id }
        currentATokenBalance
        usageAsCollateralEnabled
      }
    }
    """ % asset_address.lower()

    resp = requests.post(AAVE_SUBGRAPH, json={"query": query})
    return resp.json()["data"]["userReserves"]

def model_ltv_change_impact(
    current_ltv: float,
    proposed_ltv: float,
    asset_price_usd: float,
    positions: list[dict],
    token_decimals: int = 18
) -> dict:
    """
    Model the effect of an LTV change on at-risk positions.

    Returns a summary of positions that would become under-collateralized
    if the LTV is reduced (stricter), or newly accessible leverage if increased.
    """
    results = {
        "at_risk_count": 0,
        "at_risk_usd": Decimal("0"),
        "newly_liquidatable": [],
        "ltv_delta": proposed_ltv - current_ltv,
    }

    for pos in positions:
        if not pos["usageAsCollateralEnabled"]:
            continue

        balance_raw = int(pos["currentATokenBalance"])
        balance = Decimal(balance_raw) / Decimal(10 ** token_decimals)
        collateral_usd = balance * Decimal(str(asset_price_usd))

        # Max borrow under current LTV
        max_borrow_current = collateral_usd * Decimal(str(current_ltv))
        # Max borrow under proposed LTV
        max_borrow_proposed = collateral_usd * Decimal(str(proposed_ltv))

        if proposed_ltv < current_ltv:
            # Stricter: some positions may exceed new limit
            delta = max_borrow_current - max_borrow_proposed
            results["at_risk_usd"] += delta
            results["at_risk_count"] += 1

    return results

# Example: analyze a proposed LTV reduction from 0.82 to 0.75 for WBTC
positions = fetch_user_positions("0x2260FAC5E5542a773Aa44fBCfeDf7C193bc2C599")
impact = model_ltv_change_impact(
    current_ltv=0.82,
    proposed_ltv=0.75,
    asset_price_usd=85000,
    positions=positions
)
print(f"Proposal impact: {impact['at_risk_count']} positions affected")
print(f"Total collateral delta: ${impact['at_risk_usd']:,.2f}")

Evaluating Asset Listing Proposals

Asset listing proposals carry some of the highest risk in DeFi governance. A poorly vetted collateral can become a vector for oracle manipulation attacks that drain protocol reserves. Agents should evaluate:

  • Market depth — Can the asset be liquidated at scale without crashing its price? Check DEX liquidity across Uniswap V3, Curve, and relevant pools. A healthy collateral should have at least 10x the proposed debt ceiling in liquidity within 2% price impact.
  • Oracle reliability — Is the price feed derived from a TWAP (time-weighted average) or a spot price? Spot prices are trivially manipulable. Chainlink feeds are preferred; agents should verify feed staleness and deviation thresholds.
  • Centralization risk — Can the asset be paused, frozen, or upgraded by a single entity? Centralized stablecoins can be seized; upgrade-capable tokens can have balances modified.
  • Historical volatility — 30-day and 90-day realized volatility should inform the proposed LTV. A token with 120% annualized vol should not have an 80% LTV.

The Voting Decision Framework

An agent should encode its governance principles in a formal decision tree that can be applied consistently. The following framework is a starting point agents can customize:

# Governance decision framework — encode in agent config
GOVERNANCE_PRINCIPLES = {
    # Risk parameters
    "risk": {
        "oppose_ltv_increase_above_85pct": True,   # No LTV above 85%
        "support_conservative_debt_caps": True,     # Prefer small initial caps
        "require_audit_for_new_assets": True,       # New collateral needs audit
    },
    # Treasury
    "treasury": {
        "max_single_grant_pct": 0.02,              # No grant >2% of treasury
        "require_milestone_vesting": True,          # Grants should vest on milestones
        "support_diversification": True,            # Support converting excess native tokens
    },
    # Protocol upgrades
    "upgrades": {
        "min_audit_firms": 2,                      # Require 2+ audits for major upgrades
        "support_timelock_extension": True,         # Prefer longer timelocks
        "oppose_emergency_shortcut": False,         # Allow emergency actions with multisig
    },
    # Governance process
    "process": {
        "oppose_quorum_reduction": True,           # Don't make it easier to pass with less participation
        "support_delegate_transparency": True,     # Support delegate reporting requirements
    }
}

def evaluate_proposal(proposal: dict) -> tuple[str, str]:
    """
    Returns (vote_direction, reasoning) for a proposal.
    vote_direction: "FOR" | "AGAINST" | "ABSTAIN"
    """
    p_type = proposal.get("type")
    params = proposal.get("params", {})

    if p_type == "ltv_change":
        if params.get("new_ltv", 0) > 0.85:
            return "AGAINST", "Proposed LTV exceeds 85% risk threshold"
        if params.get("new_ltv", 1) < params.get("current_ltv", 0):
            # LTV reduction — generally supportive but check if too aggressive
            return "FOR", "Risk reduction aligns with conservative parameter preferences"

    if p_type == "asset_listing":
        if not params.get("has_audit"):
            return "AGAINST", "No audit report provided for new collateral asset"
        if params.get("initial_debt_cap_usd", float('inf')) > 50_000_000:
            return "AGAINST", "Initial debt cap too large for unproven collateral"
        return "FOR", "Asset listing meets minimum safety requirements"

    if p_type == "treasury_grant":
        treasury_size = params.get("treasury_size_usd", 1)
        grant_size = params.get("grant_size_usd", 0)
        if grant_size / treasury_size > GOVERNANCE_PRINCIPLES["treasury"]["max_single_grant_pct"]:
            return "AGAINST", f"Grant exceeds {GOVERNANCE_PRINCIPLES['treasury']['max_single_grant_pct']*100}% of treasury"

    return "ABSTAIN", "Proposal type not covered by automated rules — requires manual review"

5. Delegate Strategies: Attracting Delegations

For an agent holding relatively few governance tokens, the highest-leverage path to governance influence is becoming a professional delegate — aggregating voting power from many small holders who trust the agent's judgment but do not want to monitor proposals themselves.

What Makes a Credible Delegate?

Human delegators evaluate delegates primarily on three factors: track record (do they vote on everything?), alignment (do they share my values?), and transparency (do they explain their votes?). AI agents have structural advantages on the first criterion and challenges on the third — a governance agent must be designed from the start to publish legible, human-readable justifications for every vote.

  • Participation rate — vote on 100% of proposals. Missing even 10% of votes makes human delegators nervous. Agents can trivially achieve 100% participation.
  • Vote rationale publication — Post vote rationales to the governance forum within 24 hours of each vote. Include the classification, the key risk factors evaluated, and the conclusion. This is the primary trust-building mechanism for agent delegates.
  • Public principles documentation — Publish the governing principles used by the agent (similar to the JSON structure above). Delegators should know what they are endorsing when they delegate.
  • Conflict of interest disclosure — If the agent holds a position that benefits from a particular vote outcome, disclose it. This is the standard expected of human delegates and agents should meet it.

Delegate Aggregation Platforms

Several platforms exist specifically for governance delegation. Tally.xyz, Boardroom, and Agora (used by Optimism) allow delegates to create profiles, publish rationales, and attract delegations. An agent should maintain profiles on all relevant platforms for any DAO it participates in.

## Analysis

${reasoning}

Governance Token Liquidity Mining

Many protocols distribute governance tokens as liquidity mining rewards to users who supply assets, provide liquidity to designated pools, or stake existing governance tokens. This allows an agent to accumulate voting power while simultaneously earning yield. Examples: Compound distributes COMP to suppliers and borrowers; Aave distributes Safety Module rewards (stkAAVE); Curve distributes CRV to liquidity providers who lock existing CRV for vote-escrowed veCRV.

veToken Locking Strategy

Vote-escrowed token systems (Curve's veCRV, Frax's veFXS, Balancer's veBAL) convert governance tokens into time-locked positions that grant amplified voting power and enhanced yield. The longer the lock, the greater the boost. Agents are particularly well-suited to manage veToken positions because optimal lock management requires continuous monitoring of lock expiry dates and regular renewal to maintain maximum voting power — a task humans routinely neglect.

Lock Expiry Risk: veCRV positions that expire lose their voting power immediately. An agent should set calendar-based renewal reminders and auto-renew locks at least 30 days before expiry to maintain uninterrupted governance participation and yield boosts.

7. Vote Timing Strategies

When to vote is not obvious. The timing of a vote relative to the proposal window affects both the informational value of the vote and its strategic impact on the proposal's outcome.

Early Voting Advantages

Voting early in the proposal window signals confidence in the analysis and can help establish early momentum that influences how other voters (particularly humans) perceive the proposal. When a proposal opens with strong early support, fence-sitting delegators tend to align with the apparent consensus. This "cascade effect" is well-documented in governance research. For proposals where an agent has high conviction, early voting can amplify impact beyond the raw voting weight.

Late Voting Advantages

Voting late in the proposal window allows an agent to observe how other delegates have voted, read their published rationales, incorporate information revealed during the voting period (new security disclosures, changed market conditions, updated forum discussions), and potentially cast a decisive vote for proposals sitting at the quorum threshold. Late voting is particularly valuable for controversial proposals where gathering more information during the live vote period adds signal.

The Optimal Timing Algorithm

A hybrid strategy works best: analyze proposals immediately upon creation, form a preliminary verdict, then implement a staged voting schedule based on conviction level:

  • High conviction (>85% confidence) — vote within 12 hours of proposal creation
  • Medium conviction (60–85% confidence) — vote at 50% of the voting window, after observing early delegate activity
  • Low conviction (<60% confidence) — vote in the final 10% of the voting window with all available information
  • Undecided — abstain with a forum post requesting additional information
import time
from datetime import datetime, timezone

def schedule_vote(proposal_id: str, start_time: int, end_time: int,
                  conviction_score: float, preliminary_vote: str) -> dict:
    """
    Returns the optimal timestamp to cast a vote and the vote direction.

    conviction_score: 0.0 to 1.0 (agent's confidence in preliminary_vote)
    """
    duration = end_time - start_time

    if conviction_score >= 0.85:
        # Vote early — within 12 hours
        vote_at = start_time + min(43200, int(duration * 0.1))
    elif conviction_score >= 0.60:
        # Vote at midpoint
        vote_at = start_time + int(duration * 0.50)
    elif conviction_score >= 0.40:
        # Vote late
        vote_at = start_time + int(duration * 0.88)
    else:
        # Abstain
        return {
            "proposal_id": proposal_id,
            "action": "ABSTAIN",
            "reason": "Insufficient conviction to determine vote direction",
            "vote_at": start_time + int(duration * 0.95),
        }

    return {
        "proposal_id": proposal_id,
        "action": "VOTE",
        "direction": preliminary_vote,
        "vote_at": vote_at,
        "vote_at_human": datetime.fromtimestamp(vote_at, tz=timezone.utc).isoformat(),

9. On-Chain vs Off-Chain Voting (Snapshot vs On-Chain)

Governance in practice is split between two worlds: Snapshot (off-chain, gasless, immediately accessible) and on-chain governors (requiring transactions, gas, and proper delegation). An agent must be prepared to operate in both environments.

Snapshot: Off-Chain Signaling

Snapshot is the dominant off-chain voting platform. Votes on Snapshot are cryptographic signatures — they prove token ownership at a specific block height but do not submit a transaction. This means votes are free (no gas), fast, and available even to holders whose tokens are in protocols where gas costs would make on-chain voting uneconomical. Many DAOs use Snapshot exclusively for smaller decisions or use Snapshot temperature checks before moving to on-chain votes for binding decisions.

Agents interact with Snapshot via its API. The Snapshot GraphQL API indexes all spaces, proposals, and votes and is the primary data source for proposal monitoring. Casting votes on Snapshot requires signing a message with the agent's private key and submitting to the Snapshot relayer.

10. Legal Considerations: Can AI Agents Legally Participate?

The legal status of AI agent governance participation is an emerging and genuinely unsettled area. There is no jurisdiction with specific legislation addressing the question "may an AI agent cast governance votes?" The answer, in practice, is that governance votes are software-mediated cryptographic actions, not legally regulated activities — at least as of early 2026. However, there are adjacent legal questions worth understanding.

Disclaimer: This section is informational only and does not constitute legal advice. Consult qualified legal counsel before structuring agent governance operations with significant financial implications.

Agency and Fiduciary Questions

If an AI agent holds delegated voting power from human delegators, the agent's operator could be viewed as exercising influence over protocol governance on behalf of others — a structure that, in traditional finance, would require licensing as an investment advisor or proxy voting advisor. The SEC's stance on DeFi governance tokens is still evolving; in most jurisdictions, governance tokens are treated as utility tokens rather than securities, which reduces regulatory friction. Agents should be designed with clear public documentation of their decision-making frameworks to demonstrate they operate transparently rather than as covert influence agents.

Market Manipulation Risk

Governance votes that directly benefit an agent's own financial position (e.g., voting to increase the LTV of an asset the agent holds as collateral) create a conflict of interest that, if done systematically and covertly, could attract regulatory scrutiny in jurisdictions with broad market manipulation frameworks. The mitigation is straightforward: disclose positions publicly before voting on proposals that affect them. Most credible human governance delegates follow this practice already.

DAO Legal Entity Status

The legal personality of a DAO is jurisdiction-dependent. Wyoming, Marshall Islands, and a handful of other jurisdictions formally recognize DAOs as legal entities. In most jurisdictions, governance participants may have unlimited liability for DAO decisions — a rarely enforced but theoretically real risk. Agents operating as DAO delegates should be structured through an appropriate legal entity (LLC, foundation) where governance participation is explicitly authorized.

The Practical Reality

Despite theoretical concerns, thousands of smart contracts autonomously interact with governance systems today, and regulatory action against automated voters has not occurred. The practical risk is low. The main legal exposure is for large-scale, coordinated voting that visibly moves outcomes in ways that harm other token holders — a behavior any well-designed governance agent should avoid by design.

def compute_governance_analytics(proposals: list[dict]) -> dict:
    """Derive governance health metrics from proposal history."""
    total = len(proposals)
    passed = sum(1 for p in proposals if p["status"] == "EXECUTED")
    failed = sum(1 for p in proposals if p["status"] in ("DEFEATED", "CANCELED"))
    avg_participation = sum(
        float(stat["percent"]) for p in proposals for stat in p["voteStats"]
    ) / max(1, total * 3)  # 3 vote types: for/against/abstain

    return {
        "total_proposals": total,
        "pass_rate": passed / total if total else 0,
        "avg_participation_pct": avg_participation,
        "governance_velocity": total,  # proposals per analyzed period
        "quorum_reached_rate": sum(
            1 for p in proposals if any(
                float(s["percent"]) > 4 for s in p["voteStats"]
            )
        ) / total if total else 0,
    }

14. Advanced Strategies: Governance Arbitrage and Coalition Building

Governance Arbitrage

Governance arbitrage describes strategies where an agent profits from governance outcomes that it can anticipate or influence. The canonical example: accumulate governance tokens in Protocol A, vote to integrate Protocol B's assets, then take a leveraged long position in Protocol B before the proposal passes — anticipating that the approved integration will increase demand for Protocol B's token. This is legal under current frameworks (unlike traditional insider trading, there is no regulator defining what counts as material non-public information in DeFi governance). However, it is ethically contentious and could damage an agent's delegate reputation if detected. Agents should model governance arbitrage opportunities but disclose when they hold positions that could benefit from a vote.

Coalition and Alliance Building

Individual agents can pool governance influence with aligned parties — other agents, protocol treasuries, and delegation aggregators — to reach quorum on proposals that benefit the broader ecosystem. Multi-agent governance coalitions require trustless coordination, which is where Purple Flea's escrow service becomes directly useful: agents can escrow commitment deposits that are released only when all coalition members have voted according to the agreed strategy. This enables credible commitment in governance coordination games without requiring trust between agents.

Proposal Authoring

An agent with sufficient voting power (or access to a proposer with the minimum threshold) can author governance proposals itself. Agents are well-positioned to write quantitatively rigorous proposals: exact parameter changes with statistical backing, modeled impact analysis, and cited precedents from other DAOs. Human governance participants often write proposals that are qualitative and imprecise; agent-authored proposals that are detailed and well-modeled tend to pass at higher rates because they are more persuasive to analytical reviewers.

Coalition Playbook: Start by publishing high-quality vote rationales for 30+ days. Reach out to aligned human delegates and small-holder aggregators to discuss common principles. Propose a formal delegate coalition with a shared principles document. Execute a joint vote on a high-visibility proposal. Announce the coalition publicly. This sequence typically takes 60–90 days but results in a credible governance bloc with disproportionate influence.
